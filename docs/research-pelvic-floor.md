# Research: postpartum pelvic floor exercises by week (T-009)

Compiled 2026-11-06 to ground the week-based workout prompt (REQ-47, AC-74; REQ-33; Week 1 card in D2.txt lines 10 and 16). This is a record of what sources say. It is not medical advice and it does not propose a schedule.

- Nothing here was taken from search-result summaries alone, except where marked "search only".

## Findings by week range

Only what a source states. Where a source gives an event rather than a week (for example "catheter removed"), it is placed where the source puts it in time.

### Weeks 0 to 1

When to start:
- S2 (PDF text): "You can commence breathing exercises and pelvic floor exercises (see page 9) within the first few hours after birth (if you had a Caesarean-section, you should wait for the catheter to be removed first)."
- S2: "to start pelvic floor muscle exercises as soon as possible after you have had your baby (unless you have a urinary catheter, if so wait until it is removed and you are passing urine normally)."
- S4 (PDF text): "As soon as possible after your baby's birth, start these exercises, even if you have stitches." and "If you have a catheter, it is advisable to wait until it is removed and you are passing urine normally before starting pelvic floor exercises."
- S5 (page fetch): "We recommend starting the exercises gently the day after you have given birth."
- S6 (page fetch): "You can start these exercises from day 1 if you feel ready, but it is important to start gently and build up slowly." and "If you have a catheter, wait until it has been removed before you start your pelvic floor exercises."
- S7 (page fetch): "These exercises should be started as soon as your catheter has been removed (if you have had one)"
- S8 (page fetch): "If you had a tube (called a catheter) to help you wee, start these exercises after it's taken out."
- S3 (PDF text), "0-2 weeks after birth": "Pelvic floor muscle exercises." "Basic core exercises." "Walking – increasing distance and speed incrementally as your body feels comfortable."

How to do them and how often:
- S1 (page fetch): "Squeeze around your vagina and bladder (urethra) as if you're stopping the flow of urine or squeezing during intercourse." "It's important to keep breathing normally while you do these exercises." "Make sure you do not pull in your stomach when you squeeze." "Long squeezes – hold for as long as you can, but no longer than 10 seconds, then relax." "Aim to build up to 10 repeats of each exercise, at least 3 times a day." "You can do these exercises lying down, sitting or standing."
- S2 (PDF text): "You should do your PFM exercises at least 3 times a day. Starting in lying and sitting positions and progressing to standing and active positions such as walking and bending." Also: "Do not hold your breath. Do not clench your buttocks." and "If you are sore try to do them lying on your side".
- S4 (PDF text): "Hold at the top of the 'lift' for up to 10 seconds. Release and rest for five seconds." "Repeat as many times as you can (up to 10)." "Pull the muscles in quickly and let go again. Repeat 10 times." "Practise these exercises at least three or four times a day."
- S6 (page fetch): "To begin, aim to hold the squeeze for 5 seconds."

What to avoid:
- S1 (page fetch): "Try to avoid exercises such as sit-ups, planks and high-impact exercises to start with." "Also avoid straining on the toilet and heavy lifting."
- S5 (page fetch): "we advise not to do your exercises while emptying your bladder"
- S4 (PDF text): "Never attempt double leg lifts, as this puts too much strain on your tummy and pelvic floor muscles and could injure your back."
- S2 (PDF text): "try to avoid any activity that causes strain for the first few weeks e.g. prolonged standing, vacuuming, carrying heavy objects or bags"

When to stop or call:
- S2 (PDF text): "If you find this difficult or feel a vaginal bulging when you try to tighten your pelvic floor muscles - get help from a specialist physiotherapist." and "If you are having difficulty passing urine or you have any altered sensation of the need to go, it is important that you tell your doctor immediately."
- S5 (page fetch): "Please ensure if you have any pain, bulging of the abdominals, incontinence or heaviness in the vagina, please stop and speak to your healthcare professional"
- S1 (page fetch): "If a physical problem is bothering you, ask a GP or health visitor for help at any time."

### Weeks 1 to 6

- S3 (PDF text), "2-4 weeks after birth": "Progress your pelvic floor muscle exercises." "Progress your core exercises." "Introduce body weight exercises (such as squats, lunges, bridging etc.) or start some gentle Pilates or Yoga based exercises at home." "Post-natal Pilates and Yoga classes may prefer you to be 6 weeks post-birth before starting."
- S3, "4-6 weeks after birth": "Continue to progress your exercise duration / intensity from previous weeks." "Introduce low-impact exercises, such as the static bike and / or cross trainer."
- S3: "gentle core exercises which are safe to begin from about 2 weeks after birth, depending on how you feel. None of these exercises should cause pain or discomfort, so please ease off or stop any exercises that do."
- S7 (page fetch): "We would advise that you start with Postnatal Pilates around 4 weeks after delivery."
- S2 (PDF text): "After you have had your baby, you should aim to gradually build up your exercise tolerance over the first 6-8 weeks." "try not to lift anything heavier than your baby for at least 6 weeks." "Gradually increase the time and pace of your walking every day during the first 6 weeks".
- S2: "you can start swimming once you have had 7 days clear from vaginal bleeding/ discharge. If you have had a caesarean section you may need to wait until you have seen your GP at your six-week check"
- S5 (page fetch): "You can try swimming when your postnatal bleeding (lochia) has stopped"
- S2 (PDF text), the knack: "Draw up and tighten your pelvic floor muscles before any activity that increases the intra-abdominal pressure (coughing/ sneezing/ laughing/ bending)". S4 says the same in other words ("pulling in your pelvic-floor muscles immediately before and trying to hold them until you stop").
- S8 (page fetch), progression wording: "If 8 times is too hard, start with 4 or 6 times." and "Rest between each one until your muscles feel tired."
- S5 (page fetch): "Aim to build up slowly – contracting the muscles for no more than 10 seconds and repeat up to 10 times." (stomach exercise, stage 1) and "When you feel ready, please progress to stage 2." It "may take several weeks before you get to this stage; this is nothing to worry about."
- S1 (page fetch): "If the gap is still obvious 8 weeks after the birth, contact the GP as you may be at risk of back problems." (about the separation of the stomach muscles, not the pelvic floor itself)

### Weeks 6 to 12

- S2 (PDF text): "At your 6-8 week check with your GP, you should be reviewed for general physical and mental wellbeing. If you can, this would be a good time to have a specialist Pelvic Health Physiotherapy assessment, to support your continued post-natal rehabilitation."
- S2, return-to-running table (the PDF footer says: "this guide has been co-designed by researchers, clinicians and postpartum runners but has not yet been validated"). Text for the 6-12 week column, as extracted from a multi-column layout: "Begin integrating low impact forms of running, progressing your pelvic floor exercises and strength and conditioning." with "This may be recommended during weeks 6-12* of your return to exercise." and "*Weekly ranges are suggested as a rough guide and progress should be individual and based on symptoms, healing and how you are feeling". The same table says "Most healing occurs in the first 6-12 weeks postpartum."
- S3 (PDF text), "6-8 weeks after birth": "If you feel comfortable, you can begin some scar mobilisation (gentle massage) of any scars that you may have from birth" and "Graded implementation of resistance into your body weight exercises." "8-12 weeks after birth": "Safe to introduce swimming if any open wounds have healed appropriately."
- S7 (page fetch): at 6 weeks "It is important to progress your exercise slowly"; at 12 weeks, a sequence that prepares the body "for impact based activity such as running, jumping and resistance training."
- S6 (page fetch): "or pain from your stitches (after 6 weeks)" is listed with prolapse, bowel symptoms and pain with sex as reasons to ask a GP, health visitor or midwife for a referral.
- S4 (PDF text): "Avoid aerobics classes and competitive sports for three months. Listen to your body – stop if it hurts."
- S5 (page fetch): "Wait for 3 months before starting more strenuous aerobic or high impact exercise"

### Weeks 12 and later

- S3 (PDF text), "12+ weeks after birth": "Graded return to running and other impact exercise." Also: "We would not suggest returning to running or impact sports until at least 12 weeks postnatally. It is important to note that this is a rough guideline and not a target - some women will need to wait longer than 12 weeks before they are able to safely return to running or impact sports."
- S3 stop signs (PDF text): "You should not experience any of the following symptoms when performing any of these exercises, or any other form of exercise, such as running: Urinary or faecal incontinence (loss of control or leaking with bladder or bowels). A heaviness or dragging sensation in the vaginal area. A pendular shape / doming of the abdomen. Pain anywhere in your body." S3 also lists a readiness checklist (for example "I can walk comfortably for 30 minutes.", "I can jog on the spot for 1 minute.").
- S2 (PDF text), frequency over time: "Build up your exercise routine gradually over the weeks and months. If your muscles were weak, you should notice an improvement in 3-5 months. Then keep up the practise to maintain the improvement."
- S2, table text as extracted: "If you are no longer experiencing symptoms, you may reduce your pelvic floor exercises." with "This may be recommended during weeks 12-18* of your return to exercise." and, under the pelvic floor row, "Reduce to 1-2x weekly if no symptoms" and "Maintain and return to activity as normal". The multi-column extraction means I cannot be fully certain which phrase sits under which stage column; check the PDF page 14 before relying on the pairing.
- S4 (PDF text): "It takes three to six months to strengthen pelvic-floor muscles with regular training." "Just like any other muscle in the body, the pelvic-floor muscles will go weak again if not exercised regularly."
- S8 (page fetch): "It might take up to three months for your muscles to get strong again."
- S5 (page fetch): "We recommend that you continue to exercise the muscles daily for the rest of your life."
- S7 (page fetch): if the leaflet's symptom checklist (questions 3 to 6) is answered yes "3 to 6 months after delivery", contact the NHS Pelvic Health Physiotherapy service; "If it has been over 6 months since you have delivered your baby, please speak with your GP for a referral to the right service."
- S6 (page fetch): "It can take up to 6 months for your pelvic joints to recover post-delivery." S4 (PDF text): "Your pelvic joints can take between three and six months to return to normal, and possibly longer if breastfeeding."
- S9 (page fetch): "Like any muscle, it takes time to build them up, so consistency with pelvic floor exercises is key." and "It's a good idea to talk to a healthcare provider if you're concerned about your pelvic floor muscles." (general page, no postpartum timing).
- S10 (page fetch): "Pelvic floor muscle training was initiated between the third trimester of pregnancy up to 24 weeks postpartum." and "The frequency of pelvic floor muscle training interventions ranged from once a week to three times per day," and "This aligns with the ICS recommendation grade B, which advocates for pelvic floor muscle training to be provided to all postpartum women by a health professional,". These describe trials, not a recommended personal schedule.

## Where sources differ

- Daily frequency: S1 "at least 3 times a day"; S2 "at least 3 times a day"; S4 "at least three or four times a day"; S7 "three to four times every day" (or "when you are feeding your baby"); S8 gives "twice a day" for long and short squeezes and also "Try to do them three times a day"; S5 "24 exercises a day". S10 trials ranged "from once a week to three times per day".
- Hold length: S1 "no longer than 10 seconds"; S4 "up to 10 seconds" with 5 seconds rest; S6 "To begin, aim to hold the squeeze for 5 seconds"; S8 "8-10 seconds" and also "10 seconds, and repeat 10 times".
- Start time: S2 "within the first few hours"; S5 "the day after you have given birth"; S6 "from day 1 if you feel ready"; S4 "As soon as possible"; S7 and S8 "after" the catheter is removed. S2 says wait for the catheter after a caesarean; S4 and S2 add "passing urine normally".
- Stitches: S4 "even if you have stitches". Other sources fetched did not address it (a search summary said UCLH does; I could not fetch UCLH, so not verified).
- Swimming: S2 "7 days clear from vaginal bleeding/ discharge" (caesarean: maybe wait for six-week check); S5 "when your postnatal bleeding (lochia) has stopped"; S3 puts "8-12 weeks after birth" with open wounds healed.
- Running/impact: S3 "at least 12 weeks"; S4 and S5 "three months" for aerobics or strenuous/high-impact; S2 table puts "low impact forms of running" at weeks 6-12 and says it is not validated; S6 gives no time.
- Time to regain strength: S8 "up to three months"; S4 "three to six months"; S2 "3-5 months" if muscles were weak.
- Pilates: S3 says home gentle Pilates or Yoga at 2-4 weeks (classes may want 6 weeks); S7 says "around 4 weeks".
- The SPEC/D2 Week 1 card says to avoid abdominals, running and lifting "hasta que tu médico te dé el visto bueno (típicamente en la revisión de 6 semanas)". S1 says "avoid... sit-ups, planks and high-impact exercises to start with"; S3 lists "Basic core exercises" at 0-2 weeks and gentle core exercises from about 2 weeks. So the sources differ from a blanket "no abdominals" statement. S2 refers to a "6-8 week check", S7/S3 use 12 weeks for running.
- S10 (page fetch) states training started "between the third trimester of pregnancy up to 24 weeks postpartum". A web-search summary (not fetched, so not relied on) described the start as "first week postpartum up to 52 weeks postpartum". I did not read the last 77,081 characters of S10, so I cannot say which is right.

## Implications for the prompt (restating source content only)

- Sources tie the start to events, not a single day: catheter removed and passing urine normally (S2, S4, S6, S7, S8), and then "as soon as possible", "day 1" or "the day after" (S4, S6, S5).
- Sources name long squeezes and quick squeezes, breathing normally, not clenching buttocks, not pulling in the stomach (S1, S2, S4).
- Sources give frequency as at least 3 times a day (S1, S2) or 3 to 4 times (S4, S7); holds up to 10 seconds, up to 10 repeats (S1, S4); starting with a 5 second hold or fewer repeats (S6, S8).
- Sources describe progression as lying, then sitting, then standing and active positions (S2, S4), and adding the knack before coughing, sneezing, lifting (S2, S4).
- Sources list what to avoid early: sit-ups, planks, high-impact exercise, straining on the toilet, heavy lifting (S1); lifting more than the baby for at least 6 weeks (S2); exercising while emptying the bladder (S5); holding the breath (S2, S7); double leg lifts (S4).
- Sources give week bands for related activity: S3 (0-2, 2-4, 4-6, 6-8, 8-12, 12+ weeks) and S2 (6-8 week GP check, weeks 0-6, 6-12, 12-18, 18+ weeks, labelled rough guides and "not yet been validated").
- Sources list stop-and-contact signs: pain, bulging of the abdomen, incontinence, heaviness or dragging in the vagina (S3, S5); vaginal bulging when tightening (S2); difficulty passing urine or altered sensation (S2); wound or stitch problems, calf redness or swelling, bladder emptying difficulty at home (S7); symptoms 3 to 6 months after delivery or over 6 months (S7).
- Sources say recovery times differ by person and by mode of birth (S3, S2) and that the weekly ranges are rough guides (S2, S3).
- Where sources disagree (see the section above), a prompt would need Marina or a clinician to choose a value. This document does not choose one.
